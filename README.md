# How to Properly Block an Alpaca Wool Sweater

## A Professional Fiber Artist's Guide

---

## Why Alpaca Needs Special Care

Alpaca fiber behaves quite differently from sheep's wool during blocking. It has low crimp (2–12 crimps per inch, compared to merino's up to 100), which gives it low elasticity and low memory. This means alpaca is prone to stretching and growing when wet, and it won't spring back the way sheep's wool does. Aggressive handling or improper blocking can permanently distort the garment.

The goal is to even out stitches and set the shape *without* letting gravity or water weight pull the fibers beyond your intended dimensions.

---

## Supplies You'll Need

- A clean basin or tub
- A pH-neutral wool wash (such as Eucalan or Soak — avoid anything with enzymes)
- Several clean towels
- Rust-proof T-pins or blocking pins
- Blocking mats (interlocking foam tiles work well)
- A tape measure
- A spray bottle (for touch-ups or spray blocking)
- A blocking schematic (if working from a pattern)

---

## Step-by-Step Wet Blocking Process

### Step 1 — Measure the Dry Garment

Lay the unworn sweater flat and record its current width (bust, waist, hip) and length (body, sleeves). Compare these to your target finished measurements from the pattern or your body. This is your reference — you'll use it to avoid over-stretching during pinning.

### Step 2 — Prepare the Soak

Fill your basin with cool to lukewarm water, no warmer than about 80°F / 27°C. Hot water can relax alpaca fibers even further and accelerate unwanted stretch. Add a small amount of wool wash per the product's instructions and swirl gently to distribute.

### Step 3 — Submerge the Sweater

Gently press the sweater into the water without wringing, twisting, or agitating. Alpaca can felt when exposed to moisture and friction — its felting behavior is less predictable than sheep's wool, not simply less likely. Also avoid temperature shocking (washing warm and rinsing cold). Let it soak for 15–20 minutes so the fibers fully saturate. Alpaca absorbs a great deal of water and gets very heavy — this is normal, but it's exactly why you must support its weight carefully from this point forward.

### Step 4 — Remove Without Stretching

Drain the basin. Do *not* lift the sweater by one end — the water weight will stretch it dramatically. Instead, gather the sweater into a loose ball while it's still supported by the basin bottom, then gently press (never wring) to expel excess water. Transfer it in a bundled lump to a clean, dry towel.

### Step 5 — Roll in a Towel to Remove Moisture

Lay the sweater flat on the towel, then roll the towel up with the sweater inside, like a jelly roll. Press down along the rolled towel firmly with your hands or kneel on it to push water into the towel. Unroll, and repeat with a second dry towel if needed. Your goal is to get the sweater damp but no longer dripping, so it doesn't sag under its own weight on the blocking mats.

### Step 6 — Lay Out on Blocking Mats

Place the sweater flat on your blocking mats, smoothing it into shape with your palms. Start from the center (chest area) and work outward. Gently coax the fabric toward your target measurements — but here's the critical alpaca rule: **block to your exact target dimensions or even slightly smaller, never larger.** Alpaca will relax and grow a bit during wear, so erring on the snug side during blocking is wise.

### Step 7 — Pin the Garment

Using rust-proof T-pins, pin at key structural points: the bottom hem, side seams, underarm seams, shoulder seams, neckline, and cuff edges. Space pins every 1–2 inches along edges that need to stay straight or at a precise measurement.

For ribbing at hems, cuffs, and neckline, gently compress the ribbing inward rather than stretching it out — you want the ribbing to retain its elastic gathered look.

Use your tape measure frequently to check that both sides are symmetrical and that you're hitting your target numbers.

### Step 8 — Shape Any Details

- **Lace patterns:** Gently open up the lace motifs with your fingers or pins so the yarn-overs are visible and even.
- **Cables:** Leave as-is or lightly plump them with your fingers.
- **Colorwork:** Smooth the stitches so the floats aren't pulling and the fabric lies flat.

### Step 9 — Let It Dry Completely

This is not optional — the sweater must be *bone dry* before you unpin it. Alpaca takes longer to dry than sheep's wool because it absorbs more water. Expect 24–48 hours depending on humidity and airflow (some knitters report up to 3 days for heavier pieces in winter).

Speed the process by placing a fan nearby (not blowing directly on it with heat) or positioning the mats in a well-ventilated room. Do not move the garment or unpin it while damp; the shape hasn't set yet.

### Step 10 — Unpin and Evaluate

Once fully dry, remove pins and gently lift the sweater. It should hold its blocked shape. Check measurements one final time. If anything is off, you can lightly re-wet that specific area with a spray bottle, repin, and dry again.

## Alternative Method: Spray Blocking

For lightweight, lace, or particularly delicate alpaca pieces, **spray blocking** is a gentler alternative to full wet blocking. Alpaca fibers become very weak when fully saturated, so spray blocking reduces the risk of damage or over-stretching.

1. Lay the dry garment on blocking mats.
2. Pin it to your target dimensions using T-pins or blocking wires.
3. Mist the fabric evenly with a spray bottle of cool water until damp — not soaking wet.
4. Allow to dry completely before unpinning.

This method is especially recommended for fine-gauge or halo yarns (like brushed alpaca blends) where you want to preserve the fiber's texture.

---

## Steam Blocking: Use With Caution

Steam blocking is an accepted method for alpaca when done carefully, but requires strict technique:

- Use a steamer or steam iron held a couple of inches *above* the garment surface.
- **Never press the iron directly onto the fabric** — this can permanently damage your knitting.
- Pin the garment to shape first, then apply steam to set.
- This is best used for localized corrections (e.g., flattening a curling stockinette edge) rather than as a primary full-garment method.

## Expert Tips and Additional Notes

### On Fiber Blends

If your sweater is a blend (e.g., 50% alpaca / 50% merino), you have more forgiveness — the wool component adds elasticity and memory. Adding even 20% wool to alpaca gives the yarn less stretch and more shape retention. Still, treat blends closer to the alpaca protocol than a pure wool one.

### On Felting Risk

Alpaca fiber has short, smooth scales that don't interlock as easily as sheep's wool, but it can absolutely still felt — and some fiber artists report it felts unpredictably fast under certain conditions. Always avoid agitation, temperature shocking, and harsh handling when wet.

### On Storage

Store your blocked alpaca sweater **folded, never hung on a hanger.** Gravity is alpaca's nemesis, and a hanger will stretch the shoulders irreversibly.

### On Garment Design

100% alpaca is best suited for accessories where stretching isn't a problem — cowls, scarves, shawls, and blankets. For fitted sweaters, an alpaca/wool blend is strongly recommended by most fiber professionals to maintain shape over time.

## Fact-Check Notes

This guide was reviewed against multiple expert fiber arts sources. Key corrections and clarifications from the review:

1. **Memory and elasticity:** Alpaca has *low* memory and approximately 10% elasticity — not zero. It is significantly less elastic than merino (which has up to 100 crimps per inch), but does retain some structural memory.

2. **Spray blocking alternative:** Multiple professional sources recommend spray blocking as the preferred method for delicate alpaca fibers, since they weaken considerably when fully wet. This guide was updated to include spray blocking as a primary alternative.

3. **Steam blocking:** Expert sources confirm steam blocking is a valid method for alpaca when done with a hovering iron or steamer — never with direct contact. The original caution was slightly overstated and has been corrected.

4. **Felting behavior:** Alpaca's felting risk is less predictable rather than simply lower than sheep's wool. The smooth fiber scales make it harder to felt intentionally, but uncontrolled agitation and temperature changes can still cause irreversible felting.
